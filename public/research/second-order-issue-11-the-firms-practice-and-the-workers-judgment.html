AI, Capital & Work

The Firm's Practice and the Worker's Judgment

Entry-level performance and skill formation can diverge because they track two different boundaries.

Read the companion deck (PDF)→

SUMMARY

AI moves knowledge across the boundary between a firm and its workers, in both directions. It does not move knowledge across the boundary Michael Polanyi drew inside a worker, between what someone can state and what someone can do. Measured performance tracks the first boundary and skill formation tracks the second, so a rise in one beside a fall in the other is two readings.

Four experiments measure the first transfer, in which the firm's best practice goes to a new hire as finished AI output. Each defines a new hire a different way, and in every one the newest improved the most. Among 5,172 customer support workers at a single software firm, a worker with two months on the job and the AI performed as well as a worker with six months on the job and no AI.

Two trials measure the second transfer by taking the AI away. In both, unaided scores stayed flat or fell. Among 825 students at a Turkish high school, those who had practiced with a plain AI scored 5.4 points below those who had practiced without one, on an exam taken without the AI. Among 52 programmers learning a new tool, those who learned it with an AI understood it less well once the AI was gone.

Issue 03 found in June that the AI-exposed occupations were still growing while hiring into them froze, and this issue explains both facts. By Stanford's count, employment of 22-to-25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed jobs fell about 11 percent between November 2022 and June 2026 while the same age group in less exposed jobs grew about 10 percent, and firms that adopted AI heavily grew headcount, entry level included. The jobs are growing and the youngest are the ones not being brought in, and juniors who are hired may reach mid-level with high assisted output before unaided judgment catches up.

An AI system trained on a firm's accumulated practice can hand a new employee that practice as finished output. The judgment inside that practice is a separate transfer, and whether it moves is what this issue tests.

01

What a firm knows and where it keeps it

Take a support worker handling a refund request. The refund policy itself is written down, and anyone can follow it on their first day on the job. That is codified knowledge. Knowing which kind of caller needs the policy explained before it is quoted is the second kind, which Michael Polanyi named tacit knowledge in Personal Knowledge in 1958. That knowledge is listed in no manual, and it is learned by taking calls next to someone who picked it up by being on the job.

For each kind of knowledge there are also two places it can be stored, the worker and the firm. Richard Nelson and Sidney Winter, in An Evolutionary Theory of Economic Change in 1982, called the firm's kind organizational routines. An example is the practiced way an insurance claim moves from intake to payment: which desk it goes to next, who is asked when a case does not fit, what is checked before money leaves. The firm owns that routine. It can lose the people who ran the process while the process stays at the firm, and the reverse holds too: it can hire capable people into a process and still wait months for that process to run smoothly.

A new employee acquires the firm's kind of knowledge by doing the firm's work, which is one of the primary objectives of entry-level roles in the first place. Jean Lave and Etienne Wenger called this legitimate peripheral participation in Situated Learning in 1991. The newcomer does real work at the edge of a practice, handed over in larger pieces as they earn them. The junior drafts what a senior will check, and the checking teaches the senior's judgment and the firm's routine.

Take that work away from the new employee and they stop receiving the judgment. Matt Beane's field study of surgical training, published in Administrative Science Quarterly in 2019, compared open surgery with robotic surgery. In open surgery the trainee held the instruments for hours of each operation under supervision. In robotic surgery the senior surgeon could do most of the operation alone from the console, and the trainee's share fell so far that the approved training methods stopped working. The residents who learned the skill anyway did so by irregular routes Beane calls shadow learning, and he extended the account to other occupations in The Skill Code in 2024. The trainee's hours at the table were what the robot removed, and those hours were how judgment had been passing from one surgeon to the next.

AI substitutes most effectively for codified knowledge, the kind a college degree certifies, and complements the tacit knowledge that senior employees have built from experience. That is how Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar and Ruyu Chen explain their own employment figures in Canaries in the Coal Mine, and those figures come next.

Meanwhile an AI system trained on a firm's own records can hand the firm's routines and worked cases to a new employee as finished output on the first morning. It can also move what a senior worker owns into the firm's records as training data. No study assembled here shows that finished output becoming the employee's own judgment. A firm that puts an AI assistant in front of its juniors is therefore deciding which parts of the work they still do by hand, and that decision fixes what those juniors will know when the firm needs them as seniors (Exhibit 1).

02

Where entry employment fell

By Stanford's count, employment of workers aged 22 to 25 in the two most AI-exposed of five occupation groups fell about 11 percent between November 2022 and June 2026, while employment of the same age group in the other three groups rose about 10 percent. The exposed group ended about 19 percent short of where it would have been had it kept pace with the rest. Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen built those figures from the client records of ADP, the payroll processor, and scored each occupation's AI exposure with ratings Eloundou and coauthors made for GPT-4 (Exhibit 2). The authors call the findings descriptive, claim no cause, and follow no worker to a destination. Issue 03 reported the February revision of this paper at 16 percent on the same measure.

That figure is consistent with what Second Order found in June. Issue 03 showed AI-exposed occupations up 10.4 percent in employment from 2019 to 2025 across all ages. The rate of hiring into them had frozen. The Stanford data say the same occupations were shedding 22-to-25-year-olds while older workers in them held steady. The addendum to Issue 03 then added the firm side from Ramp's spending data on 21,559 US firms. The heaviest AI adopters ran total headcount about 10 percent above firms that had not yet adopted, and entry-level headcount about 12 percent above, averaged over the two years after adoption, though those firms were already growing faster before they adopted. The addendum's reading, which stands, is that the firms adopting AI are hiring juniors and the freeze is concentrated among the firms that are not. The Issue 03 addendum covers who is hiring. What the junior who is hired learns once the AI does the written-down part of the job is a separate measurement, and headcount series do not answer it.

Most entry work has already been written down, which is why a technology that substitutes for written knowledge would fall first on the youngest workers. That is the Stanford authors' own explanation, and they test it with two indices. Entry employment grew more slowly in occupations that rely on codified knowledge, and employment of mid-career and senior workers grew faster in occupations that rely on tacit knowledge. Once they control for how many of an occupation's workers hold a college degree, only the association with senior tacit knowledge remains attached to the employment pattern. The authors note that the college-degree control may absorb the codified-knowledge mechanism, since a degree certifies that knowledge. Both parts of the frame are in the data. Young employment fell in occupations heavy on written work, and mid-career and senior employment rose in occupations heavy on unwritten work.

The jobs in which employment of the young has fallen are the jobs whose written part an AI assistant can now supply, and the unwritten part of those jobs was learned by doing the written part first. A firm that stops hiring juniors into those jobs saves a junior's salary this year. In three years it lacks the senior that hire would have become. The Stanford figures are consistent with that trade being made across many firms at once.

03

What the AI assistant hands a new hire

An AI assistant trained on a firm's own records hands the firm's practice, meaning the way its best people do the work, to a new hire as finished output, and the newest hires gain the most from it. The largest of four experiments showing this followed 5,172 customer support workers at a single software firm as an AI assistant was rolled out. Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, who published the study in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2025, found support workers resolving about 15 percent more customer issues an hour on average, and the least skilled and least experienced resolving about 30 percent more. A worker with two months on the job and the AI performed as well as a worker with six months on the job and no AI (Exhibit 3).

The practice being handed over was the firm's own. The vendor trained the model on the conversations of the firm's top-performing support workers, and the authors say such systems may be capturing and spreading the behavior of the most productive workers. What a strong worker had learned by taking calls reached a new worker as a suggested reply on the first morning.

Some of that practice stayed with the workers when the AI went down. During later outages their call handling times stayed 15 to 25 percent below the level from before the rollout. Outages are rare and the estimates from them are noisy, which is the one qualification the evidence needs. An outage measures the same worker on familiar calls without the tool. It leaves untested any call type absent from the firm's training records.

Newer software developers improved more than longer-serving ones when an AI coding assistant was randomly given to 4,867 developers at three firms. Cui, Demirer, Jaffe, Musolff, Peng and Salz, who reported the trials in June 2025, found completed pull requests, the unit of finished code, rising 26 percent. At the one firm where tenure was measured the shorter-tenured developers improved by more, though the difference was too imprecise to be statistically firm.

Consultants whose earlier unaided work had scored lowest improved most among 758 Boston Consulting Group consultants, in a trial Dell'Acqua and eight coauthors published in Organization Science in March 2026. On tasks the AI handles well, consultants given the AI completed 12 percent more tasks and finished 25 percent faster, and quality rose most among those whose work without the AI had scored in the lower half. On a task built to fall outside what the AI handles well, the same consultants were 19 percentage points less often correct than consultants working without it. A firm that buys the AI buys both results, so the 19 points cannot be separated from the 12 and the 25.

Schooling counted for less once an AI was available, in a trial of 1,174 Argentine adults aged 25 to 45. Cruces, Fernandez Meijide, Galiani, Galvez and Lombardi gave a randomly chosen group of them an AI for an online business task, in a trial circulated in August 2026. Without the AI, the more educated outscored the less educated by a wide margin; with it, that gap closed about three quarters of the way. About a quarter of the gap remained, and the authors' own footnote declines to call the narrowing durable learning.

Those are four different ways of being new to the work: lowest prior performance among support workers, shortest tenure among developers, lowest tested ability among consultants, least schooling among adult learners. In every one, the people at the bottom improved most once they had the AI. No average of the four exists, because they measure different things.

Once an AI assistant is in place, a new hire's output no longer tells the firm what the new hire knows. The hire receives the firm's best practice as output before doing any of the work that produced it. Output and speed in the first months used to be how a manager judged who was learning the job, and those are the two measures the AI raises directly, so they now measure the tool as much as the person. What the hire has learned shows only where the tool stops, and for the consultants that was a task the AI handled badly.

04

What the worker keeps when the AI is gone

Two trials tested what a person can do after the AI assistant is taken away. In both, unaided scores stayed flat or fell. Bastani and coauthors, in PNAS in 2025, gave 825 students at a single Turkish high school four math practice sessions under one of three conditions: a plain AI, an AI tutor built to withhold answers and give hints instead, or no AI. Practice scores rose under both AI tools. On the exam taken immediately afterwards, with no AI for anyone, the students who had practiced with the plain AI scored 5.4 points lower on a 100-point scale than the students who had practiced with no AI at all. The students who had practiced with the withholding tutor scored the same as the no-AI students (Exhibit 4).

The withholding tutor that avoided the 5.4 point loss came with more inside it than software. Teachers had written correct solutions to every problem and listed the errors students were known to make, and the tutor was loaded with both before a student opened it. That preparation is expert labor, and it is the part a purchase order does not include. A firm that buys the AI assistant and skips the preparation has bought the version that cost 5.4 points.

Programmers who learned a new coding tool with an AI understood it less well when tested without one. Shen and Tamkin, in a trial circulated in February 2026, had 52 Python programmers who had never used the Trio library learn it, half with an AI assistant and half without, and then take a comprehension test with no AI. The group that had learned with the AI scored lower. Most of these programmers had seven or more years of experience, so the result is about experienced people learning one new thing, which is what a new hire does every week.

Bastani's exam and the four experiments before it do not conflict, because they measure two different transfers. The four measured output produced with the AI in hand, and it rose most for the newest. The two unaided tests measured what a person could do once the AI was gone, and it had not risen. The AI moves the firm's practice to the worker as output. Turning that output into the worker's own judgment is a different transfer, and no study here showed it happen.

A junior's output and a junior's judgment therefore have to be measured separately, because the first no longer predicts the second. A junior who produces experienced work with the AI may still be a first-month junior without it, and the firm finds out which the first time the AI is wrong on a case that matters.

05

The people who hold the practice

A model that stores a firm's practice is built from what the firm's people know, and those people have a say in what goes into it whether or not anyone asks them. Cullen, Li and Li surveyed 497 employed United States workers early in 2026 about what they know at work that is written in no company document, training material or record. Over 60 percent said they hold a lot of such knowledge about judgment, communication and problem solving, and almost 50 percent said the same about people, processes and client preferences.

Ninety-five percent of the same workers said they could withhold that knowledge from their employer if they chose. The survey did not ask whether they would want to, so the figure measures an option workers know they have. It says nothing about their intention to use it. For a firm that wants to build a model from its people's work, the figure means nearly every worker knows the material is theirs to hold back.

Once workers are told what the data is for, they hand over less. In a second sample of 971 workers, a randomly chosen group was told that worker data would be used to train an AI, and the share refusing to hand over their own past work rose from 25 percent to 41 percent. What they were asked for was their own earlier survey answers, in return for a $10 bonus, so it tests the reaction on survey data, and firm data would be the next test. The same workers, 97 percent of them, also said they could supply more than they now do.

Moving what a firm's experienced people know into a model is a negotiation with those people, and the firm is in that negotiation whether or not it conducts one. A worker who learns that a recorded call or a written ticket is training data can change what goes into it. A firm that says what the data is for will get a different record from a firm that does not, and the one survey on the question says the difference runs toward less.

06

The tasks the AI removes decide what is left to learn

The tasks an AI assistant takes out of a job decide what is left in that job for a newcomer to learn from, and the wage evidence so far says the tasks being taken are the inexpert ones. Autor and Thompson predict higher pay and a narrower qualified set after automation removes a job's least expert tasks. They predict lower pay and wider entry after automation removes the expert tasks.

Their test, across 303 census occupations from 1980 to 2018, confirms the pay half of the rule. Where automation eliminated an occupation's least expert tasks, pay in that occupation rose. The rule also says fewer people should hold those jobs afterward, and on that half the authors' own estimate is too imprecise to tell, so the headcount effect remains a prediction.

Davis at the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas tested a version of the same idea on recent wages across 205 occupations, comparing each occupation's wage growth in 2022 through 2024 with its growth in 2018 and 2019. In occupations where experience earns no extra pay, more AI exposure went with slower wage growth, about 0.28 percentage points a year for each standard deviation of exposure. In occupations where experience earns the most extra pay, AI exposure made no measurable difference to wage growth, and the estimate leans positive. Both papers report associations, and neither claims to have measured a cause.

At the entry level the first cost shows up in training, and wage effects come later. The routine tasks the AI takes from an entry role were the tasks a junior learned on. A senior used to hand a newcomer the work that could be checked, and the checking was the teaching. When the AI does that work, the newcomer is left reviewing the AI's output with no work to practice on. The firm now has to decide on purpose what its juniors practice, because the AI no longer decides it by leaving the routine tasks to the newest person in the room.

07

Where the next hire's job comes from

Most job openings over the next decade will be created by people leaving jobs, and growth in the number of jobs will create few of them. The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects about 17.5 million openings a year from 2025 to 2035. About 7.4 million a year come from people leaving the labor force entirely, for retirement or any other reason; another 9.5 million come from people moving from one occupation to another; and only about 0.6 million a year come from growth in the total number of jobs. Departures from the labor force outrun job growth twelve to one.

Even the shrinking parts of the economy keep creating openings. The four major occupational groups projected to shrink still create about 4.4 million openings a year between them, two million of those from people leaving the labor force, against a net loss of about 100,000 jobs a year. An entrant applies for the openings, and the openings in a shrinking occupation far outnumber the jobs it loses.

The projection counts openings by occupation without identifying the rung or what the person who fills the slot will learn. That depends on which tasks the firm still gives its juniors to do by hand. Issue 03 argued in June that hiring is the margin a firm cuts first, because a closed entry market costs little in the year it closes and the bill "comes due in about three years, when those hires would have become the mid-level staff the same firms then need." Juniors who were hired may reach mid-level with high assisted output and low unaided judgment, and the firm sees how many when it needs work done without the AI.

08

What would change our mind

The first thing to watch is senior review time per piece of junior work. A senior reviewing a junior's work is deciding how far to trust it, and the minutes that takes fall as the junior earns trust; that decline has always been the learning curve as the firm experiences it. If those minutes hold or rise while junior output rises, the junior is producing experienced-looking work and still cannot tell when it is wrong, so the senior is supplying the judgment on every piece, and it is not growing inside the firm. Falling minutes are the ambiguous case. They can mean trust earned, or they can mean seniors reading polished AI output less closely, which is the failure this issue describes from the other side. Falling minutes therefore prove nothing on their own and need the second indicator beside them.

The second is error rates in recently promoted cohorts, measured on work done without the AI. A cohort that learned beside an AI and then performs at the rate earlier cohorts did at the same stage would show the transfer completing on the usual schedule. A cohort that performs below its predecessors on the same unaided work would show high assisted output with low unaided judgment.

The third is time to sign-off in audit, law and medicine. Each of those professions fixes a formal moment at which a person may sign without a supervisor, and the interval from entry to that moment used to be stable. If it shortens, people are reaching independent judgment faster than before, which our argument does not predict. If it lengthens, firms, chambers and hospitals are compensating for something they see in the work.

The fourth is a learning trial larger than Shen and Tamkin's 52 programmers, run outside software, and testing weeks or months after practice ends, where the trials above tested within minutes. If people who learned with an AI match the others at that distance, the deficit measured minutes after practice was an artifact of timing, and the concern is smaller than the evidence here suggests. If the deficit persists at that distance, the knowledge transfer is not happening, and the gap it leaves widens with every cohort.

Some firms are redesigning entry roles now that AI does the routine work those roles were built around, and none has published what the redesign did to junior judgment. Boston Consulting Group's 2026 survey of senior executives, reported by Fortune on September 9, found half already seeing de-skilling in their own organizations. More than 60 percent expected it to become a material problem within five years, and more than half named slower junior development as the cause. BCG's James Tucker advises concentrating the remaining judgment work on fewer juniors so that an experience curve still forms. IBM has redesigned junior developer roles away from routine coding and is tripling graduate hiring this year. Nine founders and executives of smaller technology firms, writing in Forbes on September 3, describe having the junior write the first version, letting the AI critique it and having a senior review the disagreement, or assigning some tasks with the AI switched off. Gleb Tsipursky, a behavioral scientist writing in CPA Practice Advisor on September 1 for accounting firms automating their reconciliation work, proposes a ledger: for each workflow the firm automates, name the judgment task a junior now does instead, and record the evidence that the junior can do it alone. The two measures he suggests keeping, time to independent competence and review reversals, are the same two this issue proposed watching above, time to sign-off and error rates on unaided work. The only design tested against a control group is Bastani's withholding tutor, and that was in a high school. Those are the cases that have been written up. Any firm that has put an AI assistant in front of its juniors is running the same experiment, named or not, and the answers will differ because the practice being taught differs from firm to firm. This issue does not rank them. It gives each firm the measure for grading its own: senior review time on junior work falls when judgment is forming in the junior, and stays flat when the senior is still supplying it.

SECOND-ORDER EFFECT

An apprenticeship was financed by the apprentice. A junior's work was billed to a client or sold to a customer like anyone else's, and the same hours that produced that revenue also produced a trained senior three years later. The firm got the training as a byproduct of work it was already being paid for. When an AI does the work, billing continues as before. Training no longer arrives as a byproduct of that billed work, because nobody was trained by producing the output. Training a junior now has to be paid for separately, as hours that produce no billable work, and whether the firm still comes out ahead depends on whether the AI lowers the cost of that separate teaching by more than it raised it by removing the free version. No source cited here tests which way that nets, and none measures what the senior loses when the junior's questions stop, since explaining a judgment is how many seniors came to understand their own.
