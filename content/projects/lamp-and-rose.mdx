## The Problem

Nurse Honor Guard chapters are volunteer organizations run by retired nurses, and most run on paper sign-up sheets, email threads, cash donations, and spreadsheets on a personal laptop. The chapter leaders I talked to tracked dues in one spreadsheet, ceremony availability in a second, and training certifications in a third, and arranged ceremony coverage by email.

## What I Built

Lamp & Rose gives each chapter its own space (a subdomain or custom domain) with public pages, a member portal, and admin tools. One platform serves many chapters with strict data isolation.

The admin console consolidates everything chapter leaders were juggling separately:

**Member management:** Full roster with contact info, dues status, training certifications, and ceremony availability. Filter by who's trained, who's current on dues, who's available on a given date.

**Ceremony coordination:** Schedule ceremonies, request volunteers, track RSVPs, record attendance. Members see upcoming ceremonies in their portal and can indicate availability directly. Admins see the volunteer list for each ceremony in one place.

**Training & credentials:** Track who's completed what training and when certifications expire. Overdue credentials get flagged automatically, and leaders can export a compliance report.

**Communications:** Announcements, event reminders, dues notices. Templates fill in member names and details automatically.

## Payments

I integrated Stripe Connect so donations and dues route directly to each chapter's account. The platform takes no cut.

**Public donations:** Visitors donate from the public site with embedded checkout instead of being bounced to a third-party page. Attribution ties to the chapter at the moment of payment.

**Member dues:** Members pay through the portal. The system tracks payment history, sends renewal reminders, and flags overdue accounts. Admins see who is current on dues, and the payment record is the ledger, so chapters no longer reconcile a separate spreadsheet at month end.

## Keeping Chapters Separate

Every chapter's public site, subdomain, member portal, and payments sit behind the same tenant boundary, along with the platform management layer, and the worst possible failure is a donation reaching the wrong chapter's account. So every request names its chapter explicitly, and a request with no chapter is refused instead of falling back to a default. Routing, permissions, data access, and logging all follow that rule, and it was in the first version.

## How It Came Together

This is the first thing I built that people use outside my job. I started with an anchor chapter, one group of chapter leaders whose first requirements list was a photo of notes on a napkin. They tested each release as I shipped it, and I changed the product based on what they found.

It was also the first project where what I needed most from AI was domain knowledge rather than code. I'm not involved with Nurse Honor Guard chapters, so I used it to research how they operate: their ceremonies, training requirements, membership structures, the vocabulary they use. That research shaped the domain model and helped me ask the chapter leaders better questions when I validated against the real thing.

The architecture moved from single-tenant to multi-tenant once it was clear the anchor chapter's pain points weren't unique: every chapter juggles the same spreadsheets, email chains, and manual payment tracking. Any chapter can now get its own site, with its data kept separate from other chapters.
