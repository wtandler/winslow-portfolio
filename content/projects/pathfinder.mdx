## The Problem

The program management office ran a roughly 500-person organization's work out of Project Web App on SharePoint, and the tool could not describe the work. PWA held everything at one level, a customized form per record type, with no hierarchy connecting programs to the projects under them. Across 100 to 150 projects there were no schedules, change management lived in a separate system, and leadership had no view of what was going on in the organization.

## What I Noticed

Nobody asked for a new system; the office was used to working around the old one. In February 2020 Microsoft released Project for the Web on Power Platform, and tinkering with it showed me that Dataverse could model what PWA could not: a full hierarchy of entities, with programs, projects, and change work connected in one place. I built a proof of concept, showed it to my boss, and he let me run with it.

## The Build

This was before AI-assisted coding, and I am a program manager, not a trained engineer. I taught myself model-driven Power Platform customization from thin documentation, configured the security roles by hand for each user role, and built the system stage by stage. It launched for one team and spread because it worked, eventually reaching roughly 1,000 change managers, program managers, and sponsors across 14 divisions. I managed the seven-person vendor team that scaled the delivery and designed the reporting model to run on a single analyst rather than a team, with its output feeding C-suite planning.

## Governance Was the Hard Part

One system means one taxonomy, and every team wanted its own. I had no corporate mandate to force a single way of working, so I did not try. I built a governance team with a defined governance role, let the teams argue out what the shared processes should be, and got out of the way. That process aligned 10 business units on three operating processes the platform supported natively: enough standardization to make reporting comparable, enough ownership that the teams kept using it. The teams that invested most went furthest; one built its entire change-management process on the platform, and that process is what still runs today.

## Results

1. About 1,000 users across 14 divisions ran program and change work through one governed system, replacing a flat PWA site and per-team trackers.
2. 10 business units aligned on three operating processes through a governance model the teams themselves ran.
3. The reporting model ran on one analyst instead of a reporting team, and its output fed C-suite planning.
4. The platform outlived my ownership: I handed it off in January 2024 and it remains in production today.

## Microsoft Ecosystem Fit

Pathfinder ran on Dataverse and Power Platform inside the organization's existing identity, security, and governance. Users worked in the tools they already had, which is a large part of why adoption held.

## What Pathfinder Taught Me

Pathfinder's one real limitation became the design brief for what came after. A single system forces every team into one taxonomy at the moment data goes in, and teams resist that even when the system is good. The two [Azure DevOps Portfolio Extensions](/projects/azure-devops-extensions) I shipped in 2026 invert the model: teams work in Azure DevOps, in their own terms, and AI interprets their work into the governed taxonomy reporting needs. [Threadbase](/projects/threadbase) takes the same idea further, needing only the documents teams already produce. Three generations of one problem, and Pathfinder is where I learned it.
