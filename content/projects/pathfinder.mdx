## The Problem

The program management office ran a roughly 500-person organization's work out of Project Web App (PWA) on SharePoint, and PWA could not represent how the work was organized. It stored every record at one level, with a custom form per record type and no link between a program and its projects. Across 100 to 150 projects there were no schedules, change management was tracked in a separate system, and leadership had no single view of the organization's work.

## What I Noticed

No one liked the old system, but the office had no path to a better one. The org was management and program managers with no technical people, and there was no budget to build a tool from scratch, which in 2019 and 2020 meant an expensive vendor engagement. In February 2020 Microsoft released Project for the Web on Power Platform, and testing it showed me that Dataverse could model what PWA could not: a full hierarchy of entities, with programs, projects, and change work connected in one place. I built a proof of concept, showed it to my boss, and he let me run with it.

## The Build

This was before AI-assisted coding, and I am a program manager, not a trained engineer. I taught myself model-driven Power Platform customization from thin documentation, configured the security roles by hand for each user role, and built the system stage by stage. It launched with one team, and other teams adopted it until it reached roughly 1,000 change managers, program managers, and sponsors across 14 divisions. I managed the seven-person vendor team that scaled the delivery and designed the reporting model to run on a single analyst rather than a team, with its output feeding C-suite planning.

## Governance

One system means one taxonomy, and every team wanted its own. I had no corporate mandate to force a single way of working, and a mandate would never have held across that many teams and personalities anyway. So I recruited senior managers to chair a governance council and volunteer champions from each org to join it, and the council decided what the shared processes should be. The council aligned 10 business units on three operating processes the platform supported natively. Shared processes made reporting comparable across teams, and because the teams chose the processes themselves, they kept using the system.

One team reorganized its own process around the platform. The reporting that followed grew that team's influence in its division, let it offer change-management services to more internal organizations, and made its process the change-management standard for a third of Microsoft. That process is still in use today.

## Results

1. About 1,000 users across 14 divisions ran program and change work through one governed system, replacing a flat PWA site and per-team trackers.
2. 10 business units aligned on three operating processes through a governance model the teams themselves ran.
3. The reporting model ran on one analyst instead of a reporting team, and its output fed C-suite planning.
4. The platform outlived my ownership: I handed it off in January 2024 and it remains in production today.

## Microsoft Ecosystem Fit

Pathfinder ran on Dataverse and Power Platform inside the organization's existing identity, security, and governance. Users worked in the tools they already had, which is a large part of why adoption held.

## What Pathfinder Taught Me

Pathfinder made every team enter data in one shared taxonomy, and teams resisted that even when they liked the system. The two [Azure DevOps Portfolio Extensions](/projects/azure-devops-extensions) I shipped in 2026 let teams work in Azure DevOps in their own terms, and AI maps their work to the taxonomy reporting needs. [Threadbase](/projects/threadbase) goes further and works only from the documents teams already produce.
